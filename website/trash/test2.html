Who we are

Our Mission

Building the foundations for trustworthy, replicable science in computational biology and medical informatics.

Why it matters

A reproducibility crisis at the intersection of biology and data

Computational biology and medical informatics are transforming medicine — but their power depends entirely on whether their findings can be trusted, repeated, and built upon. Across academia, clinical research, and industry, we face a reproducibility deficit that threatens scientific progress and, ultimately, patient outcomes.

Studies in genomics, systems biology, and clinical AI routinely suffer from undisclosed preprocessing choices, opaque model configurations, proprietary or inaccessible data, and computing environments that cannot be recreated. The ReproNet Computational Biology & Medical Informatics network exists to change this — not through criticism, but through collaboration, education, and the development of practical, community-driven standards.

Open & Documented Code

Advocate for and support researchers in sharing analysis code with sufficient documentation to enable independent execution and verification.

Transparent Data Provenance

Promote standards for describing the origin, processing, and version history of biological and clinical datasets used in published studies.

Reproducible Environments

Support the adoption of containerisation, version-pinned dependencies, and computational workflow tools that allow environments to be precisely reconstructed.

Independent Validation

Facilitate replication studies, benchmarking efforts, and multi-site validation projects to assess the robustness of key findings in our fields.

Education & Culture Change

Embed reproducibility training into postgraduate curricula, laboratory onboarding, and continuing professional development across our disciplines.

Policy Translation

Work with journals, funders, and institutions to turn reproducibility best practices into enforceable, incentivised requirements.

Recent Publications

From the literature

12Jun 2025
Systematic Review

Benchmarking single-cell RNA-seq pipelines: a multi-lab reproducibility assessment

A landmark study published in Nature Methods evaluated 11 widely used scRNA-seq analysis pipelines across six independent laboratories using shared raw data. The authors found substantial variability in cell-type identification and differential expression results that was attributable primarily to preprocessing choices — including ambient RNA removal and doublet detection thresholds — rather than biological variation. The study provides a checklist of mandatory parameter disclosures for authors submitting scRNA-seq manuscripts.

Read full summary →
04Jun 2025
Original Research

Reproducibility of clinical prediction models for sepsis in ICU settings across four hospital systems

Researchers from the OHDSI collaborative tested the external validity of five published sepsis prediction models across four US hospital systems using OMOP CDM-harmonised data. Performance degraded substantially at all external sites, with AUROC dropping from a median 0.82 (development cohort) to 0.63 (external validation). The authors argue that temporal and geographic validation should be mandatory prior to publication of clinical AI models.

Read full summary →
22May 2025
Methods Paper

SnakeMake profiles for portable, reproducible genomics workflows: a community resource

A consortium of computational biologists has published a curated repository of Snakemake profiles designed to make genomics workflows portable across HPC environments, cloud platforms, and local machines. The paper, in Bioinformatics, demonstrates that standardised profiles reduce environment-related workflow failure rates by over 60% and substantially improve inter-laboratory reproducibility in variant calling benchmarks.

Read full summary →
08May 2025
Commentary

Toward FAIR clinical data for reproducible medical informatics: gaps and opportunities

A perspectives piece in The Lancet Digital Health evaluates progress on FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) data principles in clinical informatics, arguing that while technical interoperability has improved, legal and governance frameworks remain the primary bottleneck. The authors recommend a tiered data access model that balances reproducibility with patient privacy.

Network Update

ReproNet releases updated checklist for computational manuscript submission

The network's Workflow & Infrastructure Working Group has published version 2.1 of its Computational Reproducibility Checklist for manuscript authors. The updated checklist adds sections on large language model use in analysis pipelines and extends guidance on containerisation to include Apptainer/Singularity for HPC contexts. The checklist has been endorsed by three partnering journals.

Guidance & Advocacy

Recommendations & Policy

Evidence-based recommendations for the stakeholders who shape how science is funded, conducted, and published.

By Stakeholder

Select a stakeholder group

Our recommendations are tailored to the specific roles, incentives, and levers available to different parts of the research ecosystem. Select a group to explore targeted guidance.

Research institutions — universities, hospitals, and research institutes — are uniquely positioned to embed reproducibility into the everyday infrastructure and culture of science.

  • 1
    Mandate research data management plans

    Require all research projects to submit a data management plan at the outset, specifying how raw data, processed data, and analysis code will be stored, documented, and made accessible.

  • 2
    Provide shared computational infrastructure

    Invest in institutional HPC and cloud platforms with standardised software environments, reducing the burden on individual researchers to recreate computing environments independently.

  • 3
    Incorporate reproducibility training into postgraduate programmes

    Include instruction in version control, workflow management, containerisation, and data provenance as core components of doctoral training across computational and clinical disciplines.

  • 4
    Recognise reproducibility contributions in academic assessment

    Update promotion and hiring criteria to recognise and reward reproducibility-enabling activities: code sharing, dataset curation, replication studies, and methods development.

  • 5
    Establish institutional open research offices

    Appoint dedicated open research or reproducibility support staff who can advise researchers on data sharing, preregistration, and compliance with funder and publisher requirements.

Funding bodies control the conditions under which research is conducted, and are among the most powerful actors in shaping scientific norms and incentive structures.

  • 1
    Require code and data availability as a condition of funding

    Make the availability of analysis code and underlying data a mandatory condition of all grants producing computational or data-driven outputs, with explicit timelines and minimum standards.

  • 2
    Fund independent replication and benchmarking studies

    Create dedicated funding streams for replication studies, large-scale benchmarking efforts, and infrastructure to support independent verification of key findings in priority areas.

  • 3
    Include reproducibility reviewers in grant assessment panels

    Add methodological experts with specific expertise in computational reproducibility to grant review panels for computational biology and medical informatics projects.

  • 4
    Fund sustainable research software engineering

    Recognise research software as a first-class research output and fund the professional research software engineers needed to develop and maintain reusable, reproducible computational tools.

Journals and publishers gate the dissemination of scientific findings and set the evidentiary standards that determine what constitutes adequate documentation of a study.

  • 1
    Adopt mandatory code and data availability statements

    Require structured availability statements for all computational manuscripts, distinguishing between fully available, available on request, and restricted access, with clear justifications for any restrictions.

  • 2
    Implement computational reproducibility checks

    Trial and scale pre-publication reproducibility checks in which editorial staff or third-party services attempt to execute the key analyses of a manuscript using the provided code and data.

  • 3
    Require structured methods reporting using community checklists

    Adopt community-developed checklists (such as ReproNet's Computational Reproducibility Checklist) as mandatory supplementary materials for relevant manuscript types.

  • 4
    Publish replication and null results

    Formally commit to publishing well-conducted replication attempts and robustly designed studies with null results, reducing publication bias that distorts the reproducibility landscape.

  • 5
    Require external validation for clinical prediction models

    For manuscripts reporting clinical prediction models or diagnostic AI tools, make external validation on an independent cohort a prerequisite for publication consideration.

Individual researchers are the primary agents of change. The practices adopted in individual laboratories and projects aggregate into the overall culture of a field.

  • 1
    Version-control all analysis code from day one

    Use Git from the start of every project, with regular commits and meaningful commit messages. Link repositories to published papers via Zenodo or equivalent services for persistent archiving.

  • 2
    Use workflow managers and container technologies

    Adopt tools such as Snakemake, Nextflow, or WDL for analysis workflows, and use Docker, Apptainer, or conda environments with pinned dependency versions.

  • 3
    Preregister computational studies where possible

    For hypothesis-driven analyses, consider preregistering the analysis plan on OSF or a relevant preregistration platform to distinguish confirmatory from exploratory findings.

  • 4
    Write for the reader who will re-run your analysis

    Document not just what your code does, but why each step was taken, what alternatives were considered, and what the expected outputs should look like at each stage.
